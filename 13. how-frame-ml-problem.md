# Framing a Business Problem as a Machine Learning Problem

## Why This Matters

Most junior data scientists are handed narrow tasks (clean this data, tune this model) by senior team members who already decided *what* problem to solve. Career growth into senior/lead roles depends on being able to take an ambiguous **business problem** and independently convert it into a well-scoped **ML problem**. This is the skill this note is about.

## The Case Study: Netflix Churn

**Business problem:** "How do we increase Netflix's revenue?"

Revenue can be increased three ways:
1. Acquire new customers (expensive, hard — good marketing needed)
2. Charge existing customers more (risks losing them)
3. **Reduce the number of existing customers who leave** (retention)

Option 3 was chosen as the most tractable lever.

### Churn Rate — the core metric

- **Churn rate** = % of active customers who stop using the platform in a given period (e.g., monthly).
- Example: 100 active users, 2 leave next month → 2% monthly churn.
- Populations only grow when the "birth rate" (new signups) exceeds the "death rate" (churn) — same logic as population biology.
- Netflix's churn was ~4%; the goal was to bring it down to ~3.5–3.75%.

**Business problem → Math problem:**
> Reduce monthly churn rate from 4% to 3.75% over the next 6 months.

This translation — turning a vague business goal into a measurable numeric target — is the first and most important step.

## Step-by-Step Framework

| # | Step | What it means |
|---|------|----------------|
| 1 | **Type of problem** | Is it supervised or unsupervised? Within supervised: regression or classification? |
| 2 | **End product** | What will actually be built/shipped? What decision or action does it drive? |
| 3 | **Check existing solutions** | Has this problem been solved before (internally or industry-wide)? Learn from it instead of starting from zero. |
| 4 | **Data required** | What raw data and features are needed? Is it actually available? |
| 5 | **Metrics to track** | How will you know the solution is working? Define metrics for the team, not just the model. |
| 6 | **Online vs. batch (offline) learning** | Will the model retrain continuously on live data, or be retrained periodically (e.g., weekly)? |
| 7 | **Check assumptions/constraints** | Feature availability, whether one global model works across all geographies/segments, etc. |

### Step 1 — Type of problem (worked example)

- First instinct: predict **whether** a customer will churn → looks like **classification** (leave / stay).
- Refinement (after team discussion): not all "likely to churn" customers are equally likely to churn. Treating them all the same (e.g., same discount) wastes money on low-risk customers.
- Better framing: predict a **churn probability score** (0 to 100%) per customer, and scale the intervention (e.g., discount size) to that score.
- This reframes it from **classification** → **regression** (predicting a continuous probability/propensity).

### Step 2 — End product

- If the task is to *reduce churn*, the deliverable isn't just a churn prediction — it's an **intervention system**: e.g., identify at-risk customers early enough to act (offer a discount, fix a content/experience gap, etc.) before they leave.
- Possible interventions considered:
  - Immediate discount on next month's subscription
  - Diagnosing the root cause (bad recommendations, buffering/network issues, missing content) and fixing that instead

### Step 3 — Check existing solutions

- Talk to people/teams who've solved similar problems before (even at ~85–95% accuracy).
- Understand what features/factors they used — this gives a head start instead of reinventing the approach from scratch.

### Step 4 — Data & features

Think concretely about what raw signals would predict churn, e.g.:
- Total watch time per month
- Time spent browsing/searching vs. actually watching
- Number of searches with no results ("I searched for X and Y, got nothing")
- Number of times a video/show was started and abandoned partway
- Click-through rate on recommendations

These are hypotheses about *why* someone might leave (poor content match, technical friction, etc.) — turned into measurable features. Getting this data usually requires working with a **data engineer** to pull it from the data warehouse, since raw production databases aren't something a data scientist queries directly.

### Step 5 — Metrics

- Compare predicted churners vs. customers who actually churned — check the gap (e.g., predicted vs actual differing by only ~0.25 is a good result).
- Metrics matter for the *whole team*, not just model validation — they act as a "north star" guiding whether ongoing work is on track.

### Step 6 — Online vs. offline learning

- **Online learning:** model updates continuously as new data streams in.
- **Batch/offline learning:** model is trained on a snapshot, deployed, and periodically retrained (e.g., weekly).
- Online learning is attractive but often **harder in practice** — behavior is volatile (holidays, seasonality, unpredictable spikes) and infrastructure needs to support continuous data flow into the warehouse and continuous retraining/output monitoring.
- Often a pragmatic middle ground is chosen: retrain in batches (e.g., weekly) rather than true online learning.

### Step 7 — Assumptions & constraints

- Are the features you assumed exist actually available? (Confirm with data engineering.)
- Does a **single global model** work across regions, or does behavior vary enough by geography that region-specific models are needed?
- Other domain-specific constraints should be identified before building anything.

## Key Takeaway

> Think before you code. Sit down and reason through the *entire* problem — type of problem, end product, existing solutions, data, metrics, learning mode, assumptions — before writing a single line of code.

This matters more at larger companies, where:
- Compute and engineering time is expensive (people are often billed per hour/resource).
- Course-correcting after building the wrong thing is costly and slow.
- Demonstrating this structured thinking — not just coding ability — is what tends to separate people who get promoted into leadership from those who stay in execution-only roles.

## Quick Reference: The Framework as a Checklist

- [ ] What is the business goal, in plain language?
- [ ] Can I express it as a measurable numeric target (math problem)?
- [ ] Supervised or unsupervised? Regression or classification?
- [ ] What is the concrete end product / action this enables?
- [ ] Has someone solved something similar before?
- [ ] What data/features do I need, and do they actually exist?
- [ ] What metrics tell me (and my team) this is working?
- [ ] Online or batch learning — and is online realistic given my data/infra?
- [ ] What assumptions (feature availability, one-model-fits-all, etc.) need validating?
